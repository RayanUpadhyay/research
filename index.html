Fairness and Dependency Preservation in Synthetic Credit Data

Rayan Upadhyay · TISB (The International School Bangalore), India🥇 Gold CREST Award

Can synthetic data safely stand in for real data when you train a loan approval model, without breaking the relationships between applicant features or making the model less fair?

In a minute

The short version

01 · THE PROBLEM

Features are tangled together

Loan approval models rely on applicant features that depend on each other. Even without sensitive attributes, correlated features can still lead to unfair outcomes, so synthetic data has to keep those relationships intact.

02 · WHAT I DID

Five datasets, one pipeline

On the German Credit dataset, I compared a model trained on the original data with models trained on data from SMOTE, CTGAN, TVAE and Gaussian Copula, measuring ROC AUC, three fairness metrics and a Dependency Preservation Score I defined.

03 · WHAT I FOUND

The real data held up best

The baseline kept the relationships between features and gave the most stable fairness-utility trade-off. The synthetic methods distorted those relationships and introduced instability, and no single method beat the baseline.

Abstract. In this study, a baseline ensemble model- trained on the original German Credit dataset without synthetic augmentation- showed the most stable fairness-utility trade-off whereas SMOTE introduced the largest distortions. This study focuses on various techniques for generating synthetic data while preserving feature interdependence during fairness-aware preprocessing of loan approval models. Loan approval models rely on interdependent applicant features. Even without sensitive attributes, correlated features can induce unfair outcomes, making dependency preservation critical. Models were trained on the German Credit dataset without synthetic augmentation and compared against SMOTE, CTGAN, TVAE, and Gaussian Copula on the basis of ROC AUC and standard fairness metrics (comparative analysis only included methods that produced valid synthetic datasets). While the baseline consistently maintained interdependencies and provided stable fairness-utility trade-offs, synthetic methods distorted interdependencies and introduced instabilities, with no single method outperforming the baseline.

The metrics

What the fairness numbers mean

Move the sliders to build your own two groups and see how each metric reacts. The marks show where the paper's three valid methods landed on the same scale.

Disparate Impact

The ratio of desired outcomes (here, approvals) between the protected and unprotected groups. Closer to 1 means fairer.

Predictive Parity

The difference in positive predictive value between groups: of the applicants the model flags, how often it's right in each group. Lower absolute differences mean better parity.

Equalized Odds

Measures differences in error rates (true positive and false positive rates) across groups. Lower values mean more equitable performance. The baseline scored 0.081 ± 0.007, SMOTE 0.118 ± 0.013 and CTGAN 0.102 ± 0.011.

Which attributes were tested: Disparate Impact and Equalized Odds were computed mainly for personal_status_sex and foreign_worker; Predictive Parity and Equalized Odds for credit_history and employment_since. The first category of each attribute is the reference group for ratio-based measures.

Dependencies

The relationships synthetic data has to keep

Dependency Preservation Score

How the paper defines it:
  1. For numerical features, Spearman correlation coefficients were computed for all feature pairs between the original and synthetic datasets, and their mean is the numerical dependency score.
  2. For categorical features, Cramér’s V was computed for all feature pairs, and its mean is the categorical dependency score.
  3. The final score averages the two. A companion measure, the Frobenius norm of the difference between the original and synthetic Spearman matrices, gives the overall distance; lower is better.

The heatmap covers the seven numeric columns only. The paper's score also includes the categorical columns through Cramér’s V.

Class balance

Why synthetic data was used at all

The data is imbalanced: most applicants have good credit. SMOTE, CTGAN and TVAE were applied to balance the classes to close to 50/50, while the baseline was trained on the original data.

The paper flags this as a limitation: the baseline was trained on imbalanced data and the synthetic methods on balanced data, so the baseline’s apparent fairness advantage could partly reflect the lack of any balancing. A class-balanced baseline would be a fairer comparison.

Honesty section

Limitations and what’s next

  • TVAE and Gaussian Copula failed. Both produced NaN for every metric because the synthetic data itself was invalid (for example constant columns, empty subgroups or undefined values), so they were left out of the comparison.
  • Small dataset. German Credit is small and not generalizable. Models trained on 800–1120 samples and were validated on 200–280, so a few errors can move the fairness estimates.
  • No significance testing. Results are means and standard deviations over 10 runs, so they should be read as trends.
  • Imbalanced baseline. The baseline used the original 70/30 data while the synthetic methods were balanced, which is not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Default generator settings. CTGAN and TVAE used default architectures and hyperparameters, with no tuning.
  • One selected model. Results come from the single model AutoGluon selected (XGBoost), not an averaged ensemble.
  • Messy attributes. The UCI version has known label problems: foreign_worker is mislabeled, and personal status and sex are combined into one attribute, so fairness numbers for these need care.
  • No privacy check. Privacy leakage, such as membership inference, was not measured.
  • Next: larger and more diverse datasets, the corrected South German Credit data, differentially private synthetic data, and combining Gaussian Copula for linear dependencies with CTGAN or TVAE for nonlinear ones.

Bottom line from the paper: for fairness-critical applications like loan approval, it may be safer to rely on the original data with fairness-aware preprocessing than on synthetic data generation, which introduces dependency distortions and fairness instability, albeit improving class balance. The findings come from one small dataset and won’t automatically generalize to other domains.
